# 03 - Positioning, Differentiation and Offer

Positioning decides how the market understands you relative to the alternatives. The offer decides whether buying is a good deal. Both come before copy. If a business is hard to describe or sounds like everyone else, no headline will fix it.

## 1. Awareness levels (Eugene Schwartz)

Decide where the target reader is. Write the headline for that level.

| Level | Reader state | Lead with | Example (language school) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Unaware | Feels a vague problem, no name for it | A story, a symptom, a surprising fact | "Why you freeze when your boss asks a question in English" |
| 2. Problem aware | Knows the problem, not that solutions exist | Name the problem sharply, show the cost | "Your English is good enough to read, not good enough to get promoted" |
| 3. Solution aware | Knows solutions exist, not yours | The outcome and the mechanism | "Speak confidently in 12 weeks with daily 15-minute live calls" |
| 4. Product aware | Knows you, comparing | Differentiate, proof, offer | "Why 300 Kampala professionals chose us over self-study apps" |
| 5. Most aware | Ready, needs a nudge | The offer, price, deadline, ease | "Enrol by Friday: first week free, pay by MoMo" |

Cold traffic (ads, social) is mostly levels 1 to 3. Warm lists, retargeting and search for the brand name are levels 4 to 5. Do not give a level-1 reader a price-and-buy-now headline, and do not give a level-5 reader a long story.

## 2. Market sophistication

How many similar promises has the market already heard?

1. **First to claim:** simple, direct claim wins ("Cheap data bundles").
2. **Claims are copied:** enlarge the claim (faster, bigger, more).
3. **Claims are saturated:** introduce a new **mechanism**, the reason it works ("the only bundle that rolls over unused data").
4. **Mechanisms are copied:** make the mechanism more specific, more credible, more demonstrable.
5. **Market is exhausted and sceptical:** lead with identification, personality and a specific audience ("for night-shift nurses"), or a radically different offer.

Most local categories (salons, shops, schools, phone sellers) sit at stage 3 to 5. Bigger claims do not work there; **specific mechanisms, specific people and visible proof do.**

## 3. Positioning components (April Dunford style)

Work through these in order and write each as a short answer:

1. **Competitive alternatives:** what would customers do if you did not exist? (Include doing nothing and informal options.)
2. **Unique attributes:** what do you have that the alternatives lack? (features, capabilities, process, people, location, speed, guarantee, access)
3. **Value:** what does each attribute let the customer achieve or avoid? (The "so what?" for each attribute.)
4. **Best-fit customers:** who cares most about that value? (Narrow beats broad.)
5. **Market category / frame of reference:** what context makes your value obvious? ("Cheaper than X" and "Better than X" are different frames; choosing the frame chooses the comparison.)
6. **Relevant trends** that make now the right time (optional).

## 4. Positioning statement and "only" claim

Template:
> For **[best-fit customer]** who **[trigger / need]**, **[Brand]** is the **[category / frame]** that **[primary value]**. Unlike **[alternative]**, we **[key differentiator + proof]**.

The one-line "only" claim:
> We are the only **[category]** that **[specific, provable thing]** for **[specific people]**.

If you cannot finish the sentence with something specific and provable, you do not have a position yet; go back to attributes and proof.

## 5. Finding the angle

An **angle** is the specific way into the promise. Generate at least 5, then choose 3 for testing.

| Angle type | Question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | What result do they want? | "Pass your resit" |
| Pain / enemy | What do they hate? | "No more power-cut surprises" |
| Speed | How fast? | "Fixed in 1 hour" |
| Effort | How easy? | "Order by WhatsApp, we do the rest" |
| Cost / value | How cheap or worth it? | "Same quality, half the price" |
| Risk | How safe? | "Pay only when it works" |
| Status / identity | Who do they become? | "For ambitious final-year students" |
| Specialisation | Who is it for? | "Barber for school-uniform haircuts, 7 minutes" |
| Mechanism | Why does it work? | "Roots-first treatment, not surface shine" |
| Story / origin | Who are we? | "Built by a nurse tired of unreadable prescriptions" |
| Community | Who belongs? | "Join 4,000 Kampala sellers" |
| Contrast | Why not the other way? | "Stop paying 150k for a book you use once" |
| Seasonal / trigger | Why now? | "Before the new term" |
| Locality | Where? | "Behind Garden City, 2 minutes from the stage" |

Pick the angle where **customer desire + competitor gap + your proof** overlap.

## 6. Stand-out tests

Run every candidate position through these:

- **Swap test:** put a competitor's name in your headline. Still true? Then it does not differentiate.
- **"So what?" test:** keep asking "so what?" until you reach a customer outcome.
- **"Prove it" test:** can you show evidence in 10 seconds?
- **"Only" test:** can you say "only we ..." truthfully?
- **Reverse test:** could a competitor credibly say the opposite? ("We are bad quality" is absurd, so "high quality" is not a position.)
- **Child test:** can an eleven-year-old repeat it after one read?
- **Remember test:** will a customer repeat it to a friend as a recommendation sentence?
- **Price-of-switching test:** why would someone leave their current habit?

## 7. Positioning commodity and local businesses

Most small businesses sell the same thing as the next shop. Ways to separate (pick what is true):

1. **Niche down:** from "salon" to "natural hair salon for locs and twists". From "shop" to "school uniforms and stationery for the term, delivered".
2. **Own an outcome, not a service:** not "dental clinic", but "pain-free root canals, booked same day".
3. **Own a time or speed:** "open until midnight", "ready in 20 minutes", "same-day delivery before 5 p.m.".
4. **Own a place or ritual:** "the café near the campus where final-year students write their projects".
5. **Make the process visible:** show the kitchen, the lab, the workshop, the checklist.
6. **Package into a named product:** "The Weekend Wedding Package", "The Starter Shop Kit". Named packages beat hourly rates.
7. **Flip the risk:** guarantee what competitors will not.
8. **Add a surprising service:** free tea, free fitting, free delivery over a threshold, WhatsApp updates.
9. **Leverage trust assets:** years in business, registered company, regulator numbers, real address, owner's face.
10. **Own a community:** WhatsApp group, loyalty club, student discount, church or association partnership.

## 8. Naming and taglines

Names: short, easy to spell on a phone, easy to say on radio, works in the local language, no embarrassing meanings, domain and handle available, signals category or benefit when possible.

Taglines: one idea, 3 to 7 words, outcome or identity, no clichés ("Quality you can trust"). Test: does it still sound meaningful on a boda-boda sign? Offer 5 candidates in different registers (benefit, identity, challenge, proof, playful).

## 9. Offer engineering

Value equation:

> **Perceived value = (Dream outcome x Perceived likelihood of success) / (Time delay x Effort and sacrifice)**

To raise value: make the outcome bigger and clearer, prove it will work, shorten time-to-result, reduce effort. To raise the offer without cutting price, change the **stack**, not the discount.

Build the offer in this order:

1. **Core promise:** the primary result in one sentence.
2. **Deliverables:** exactly what they get, named and described.
3. **Remove effort:** done-for-you elements, delivery, setup, onboarding, templates.
4. **Speed:** fast-start bonus or guaranteed timeline.
5. **Risk reversal** (section 11).
6. **Bonuses** that solve adjacent problems and cost you little.
7. **Price and payment structure** (section 10).
8. **Urgency / scarcity** that is real (section 12).
9. **Name the offer** so it is easy to ask for.

Test the offer with: "Would a smart skeptic say yes to this on a Tuesday without a discount?" If not, fix the offer before writing.

## 10. Price presentation

- **Anchor** against the real alternative or the cost of the problem ("A single textbook costs 150,000; the full revision pack is 25,000").
- **Break down** into small frequent units when truthful ("less than 2,000 a day").
- **Show total value, then price,** only if the values are real and itemised.
- **Tier** into 3 options; the middle should be the intended choice (anchor-decoy-target), with clear differences.
- **Offer payment ease:** mobile money, installments, deposit then balance, pay on delivery, school-fee-friendly timing, salary-day timing.
- **Avoid false discounts:** never invent a "was" price. If you run a sale, use the real previous price and a real end date.
- **Show what is not included** to prevent surprise and chargebacks.
- **Premium positioning:** raise price with specifics (experience, outcome, materials, guarantees), reduce discount language, increase service detail.
- **Budget positioning:** lead with the total cost of ownership, not just the number.

## 11. Risk reversal and guarantees

Pick the strongest guarantee the business can honour:
- **Money-back** within N days, no questions.
- **Result-based:** "If you do not get X, we do Y free / you pay nothing."
- **Pay after:** pay on delivery, pay after the first session, pay after inspection.
- **Trial / sample:** first week free, free fitting, free taste, free audit.
- **Replacement:** "If the file does not open, we replace it within 1 hour or refund."
- **Price match / beat.**
- **Service-level:** "Ready on time or the next one is free."

Write the guarantee in plain words, with a time limit and the exact process. Vague guarantees increase suspicion.

## 12. Bonuses, scarcity and urgency (honest versions)

Allowed:
- Real deadlines (term start, event date, price rise on a stated date, stock arrival).
- Real capacity limits (appointments per day, class seats, batch size).
- Real seasonality ("order before the rains"; "before school reopens").
- Early-bird pricing that actually ends.

Not allowed: fake countdown timers that reset, invented stock numbers, fake "others are viewing", fake last-chance repeated forever.

Bonuses: choose items with high perceived value and low cost (a checklist, a template, a priority slot, a consultation, a care guide, a WhatsApp support window). Give each bonus a name, a value claim only if provable, and a reason it exists.
